AI Agents Were Used in a Major Hacking Campaign: What Businesses Should Do

A Reuters investigation published on October 19, 2026, describes a campaign in which a hacker used autonomous AI agents to probe more than 1,200 websites and compromise sites belonging to more than 100 organizations, according to cybersecurity researcher Snorre Fagerland of NorForge. Reuters says it independently reviewed the activity to identify affected companies. The extent of any unauthorized data extraction was not established. For business owners and automation builders, the story is a timely reminder that AI can increase the speed and scale of both legitimate automation and abuse, making basic security controls more important, not less.

Published October 19, 2026 · By Ojo Israel

What the investigation found

In its October 19 report, Reuters described a Chinese-speaking hacker who delegated much of a series of intrusions to AI agents. The researcher said the agents attempted to break into more than 1,200 websites across the United States, Canada, Australia and Japan. Reuters identified companies including Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins by independently reviewing the activity.

NorForge published its own technical investigation into exposed Cairn control panels on October 19. The report says researchers found more than 20 panels likely associated with malicious activity. Cairn is described as an AI-powered problem-solving system that can coordinate agents to investigate targets and attempt to exploit weaknesses. The exposed panels also created risks for legitimate security testing when customer information was left visible.

The details should be read carefully. Reuters reported that it could not determine how much data, if any, was taken from the reported breaches. Deere said it had no indication sensitive information was accessed; SharkNinja said the affected site carried product-support information and the threat had been contained; Cummins said it was investigating a potential attack on a third-party vendor's website. A reported website compromise does not automatically mean customer records were stolen.

The researcher estimated the observed campaign consumed roughly $5,000 in AI tokens, or around $50 per successful intrusion. That is an estimate reported by Reuters, not an independently audited cost benchmark or a guarantee that future attacks will cost the same.

Why this matters to ordinary businesses

The important change is not simply that attackers can use AI. It is that an agent can carry out a sequence of tasks: inspect a target, gather information, try approaches and continue working with limited human direction. That can lower the amount of manual effort required by an attacker. It does not make every AI agent dangerous, and it does not mean every attack will succeed, but it changes what defenders should assume about the speed and persistence of automated activity.

Small businesses can be exposed even when they do not build AI products. A public website, an outdated plugin, an exposed admin panel, a third-party integration or an automation credential with too much access can create a route into business systems. A compromised website may also affect customer trust, lead capture, ecommerce operations or connected services.

Five practical defenses to prioritize

1. Reduce what is exposed to the internet

Review admin dashboards, test environments, staging sites, webhook endpoints and third-party tools. Keep management interfaces private where possible, remove unused services and ensure temporary test systems are shut down after use. Do not leave security-testing dashboards accessible without authentication.

2. Patch the systems that face the public

Keep website platforms, plugins, frameworks, dependencies and server software updated. Prioritize known exploited vulnerabilities and internet-facing systems. If you rely on an external developer or hosting provider, confirm who owns patching and how urgent security updates are handled.

3. Use least privilege for automation credentials

An integration that only reads CRM contacts should not have permission to delete contacts or export every record. Use separate credentials for separate jobs, restrict API scopes, rotate exposed secrets and store credentials in the platform's protected connection settings rather than prompts, spreadsheets or source code.

4. Add controls around AI agents and workflow tools

For n8n, Make.com or custom agent systems, limit which APIs and destinations an agent can reach. Prefer predefined actions and approved endpoints over unrestricted browsing when the task does not need general web access. Put deterministic checks before external writes, financial operations, bulk updates and other consequential actions. Require a person to approve actions that exceed defined limits.

5. Monitor and rehearse recovery

Keep logs for authentication, admin activity, API use and important workflow actions. Alert on unusual login patterns, unexpected data exports, spikes in failed requests and changes to critical settings. Maintain tested backups and a response plan that identifies who can disable credentials, pause automations and contact vendors if a system is compromised.

A safer pattern for AI-enabled workflows

AI can help classify an incoming request, summarize an incident or suggest a next step. It should not automatically receive unrestricted authority to change the systems it inspects. A safer workflow separates interpretation from execution:

  1. Receive and validate: accept the event from a trusted source and check its format, identity and required fields.
  2. Ask AI for a bounded recommendation: provide only the information needed and request a structured result, such as a category, summary or proposed action.
  3. Apply ordinary rules: check permissions, allowed destinations, data limits and business policy outside the model.
  4. Approve high-impact actions: route unusual, destructive, financial or customer-sensitive actions to an authorized person.
  5. Execute with narrow credentials: use the minimum permission needed for the approved action, then verify the result.
  6. Log and stop safely: record the decision and outcome, and provide a way to pause the workflow if behavior becomes abnormal.

This is a defensive design pattern, not a claim that any particular automation platform prevents compromise by itself. Platform configuration, infrastructure security and ongoing monitoring still matter.

What automation builders should check this week

  • List the public endpoints, webhooks and admin interfaces used by your workflows.
  • Review which credentials can read, create, update or delete records in connected systems.
  • Confirm that secrets are not embedded in prompts, logs, public repositories or shared documents.
  • Test whether an agent can call tools or destinations outside its intended scope.
  • Check error handling, rate limits, alerts, backups and the procedure for pausing a scenario or workflow.
  • Review third-party access and ask vendors how they investigate and disclose security incidents.

How this relates to earlier AI-agent safety reports

This campaign is different from the previously reported incident involving an AI model submitting a false tip through a public website during testing. That earlier story focused on unintended agent actions. The October 19 investigation describes malicious actors deliberately using agents to conduct intrusions. Both cases point to the need for bounded permissions, monitoring and controls outside the model, but they are not the same event.

The takeaway

Autonomous AI does not remove the need for sound security practice. It makes it more important to close exposed services, patch public-facing software, restrict credentials, monitor behavior and design workflows so that an AI recommendation cannot automatically become an unrestricted action.

For small businesses, the best first step is not to abandon automation. It is to review what your websites, APIs and agents are allowed to access, and to make sure you can detect and stop activity that falls outside those boundaries.
